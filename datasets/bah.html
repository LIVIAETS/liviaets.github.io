BAH — Ambivalence / Hesitancy Recognition in Videos

A first multimodal, subject-based video dataset for recognizing ambivalence and hesitancy (A/H) in real-world videos, designed for personalized digital behavioural-change interventions.

Abstract

Recognizing complex emotions linked to ambivalence and hesitancy (A/H) can play a critical role in the personalization and effectiveness of digital behaviour-change interventions. These subtle and conflicting emotions are manifested by a discord between multiple modalities, such as facial and vocal expressions and body language. Although experts can be trained to identify A/H, integrating them into digital interventions is costly and less effective. Automatic learning systems provide a cost-effective alternative that can adapt to individual users and operate seamlessly within real-time, resource-limited environments. However, there are currently no datasets available for the design of ML models to recognize A/H.

This paper introduces a first Behavioural Ambivalence/Hesitancy (BAH) dataset collected for subject-based multimodal recognition of A/H in videos. It contains videos from 224 participants captured across 9 provinces in Canada, with different ages and ethnicities. Through our web platform, we recruited participants to answer 7 questions, some designed to elicit A/H, while recording themselves via webcam and microphone. BAH amounts to 1,118 videos for a total duration of 8.26 hours with 1.5 hours of A/H. Our behavioural team annotated timestamp segments indicating where A/H occurs, providing frame- and video-level annotations with A/H cues. Video transcripts and their timestamps are also included, along with cropped and aligned faces in each frame and a variety of participant meta-data.

Additionally, the paper provides preliminary benchmarking results for baseline models at frame- and video-level recognition with mono- and multi-modal setups. It also includes results on zero-shot prediction and personalization using unsupervised domain adaptation. The limited performance of baseline models highlights the challenges of recognizing A/H in real-world videos. The data, code, and pretrained weights are available on GitHub.

Capture & annotation

Participants used our web platform to record themselves answering seven questions, some specifically designed to elicit ambivalence and hesitancy. The behavioural team then annotated timestamped segments at both frame and video level, with the underlying A/H cues. Aligned face crops and time-aligned transcripts accompany every video.

Variability & demographics

The dataset approximates the demographic distribution of sex and provincial representation in Canada. Participants span nine provinces — 31.2% from British Columbia, 20.5% from Alberta and 16.5% from Quebec — and a wide age range from 18 to 66 years. By sex, 59.8% are male and 39.3% female; by ethnicity, 52.2% White, 21.0% Asian, 11.6% Mixed and 10.3% Black. Notably, 65.2% are not students, limiting common recruitment bias.

Each participant could record up to seven videos (96 recorded all seven), averaging ~5 videos/participant and ~2.84 videos with A/H. In total there are 1,274 A/H segments across 376 videos with multiple segments and 259 with a single segment. Segments are brief — on average 4.25 ± 2.47 seconds (102.92 ± 59.16 frames) — ranging from a single frame (0.01 s) to 23.8 seconds.

Experimental protocol

Splits. The dataset is divided by participant into train, validation and test sets — train+validation hold 3/4 of participants and test holds 1/4. All videos from a participant belong to a single set, enabling subject-based learning scenarios. Ready-to-use splits are provided at video and frame level.

Imbalance. The data is highly imbalanced — at frame level only 18.15% contains A/H — which must be accounted for during training and evaluation.

Metrics. With the positive class indicating presence of A/H, we report the F1 score of the positive class, weighted F1 (WF1), and average precision (AP) of the positive class (threshold sweep 0–1, step 0.001). Evaluation code is provided.

Conclusion

BAH is a new and unique multimodal, subject-based video dataset for A/H recognition. Recruited participants answer seven designed questions to elicit A/H while recording via webcam and microphone, annotated by our behavioural team at video and frame level. Initial benchmarking yields limited performance, highlighting the difficulty of A/H recognition — and showing that leveraging context, multimodality and adapted feature fusion is a promising first direction. The dataset and code are public.
